   The State, Mr. Kohberger, and Intervenors sh are many views on audio/visual coverage of 
this case. All agree that the Court ultimately has di scretion to decide whether to allow audio/visual 
coverage. Nobody seems to dispute that most members of the impacted communities would be 
unable to observe the proceedings with their own ey es and ears absent audio/visual coverage or 
that there would be many benef its for the public and the crimin al justice system if impacted 
members of the community could 
receive contemporaneous, complete, and objective evidence about 
this case as it is presented in the courtroom. N onetheless, the State flags some concerns about 
audio/visual coverage while largely deferring to the Court and Mr. Kohberger more forcefully objects.  Idaho Court Administrative Rule 45 largely addresses the concerns that the State and Mr. Kohberger raise. Some witnesses may be acutely affected by audio/visual coverage, but those 
concerns can be addressed as they come up and on an individualized basis. Idaho Ct. Admin. R. 45(d).  
The jury should hear only the evidence or ar guments submitted to them, but Rule 45 regulates 
audio/visual coverage such that effectively only those aspects of the proceedings open to the public in the gallery are broadcasted. Intervenors Supplemental Memorandum on Camera Access in the Courtroom (“Opening Mem.”) at pp. 6–7. Regardless of whether the Court allows audio/visual 
coverage, there is going to be large amounts of publicity surrounding this case. There is no greater risk 
that potential jurors (for pre-trial proceedings) or juro rs (for trial) will be unfairly biased if there is  
Reply Memorandum on Camera Access in the Courtroom - 3 audio/visual coverage, as any information shared in open court that is not broadcasted may still be 
published by reporters who attend the proceedings. For that reason, the Court will certainly instruct the 
jurors not to watch news coverage regardless of whether it allows audio/visual coverage. 
Audio/visual coverage will not create a distraction in the courtroom. As Judge Boyce noted in 
Lori Vallow’s case: “[T]he Court affirms that ther e is no indication that any orders relating to the 
conduct of the media during hearings in this case, or the companion case, Fremont County Case No. CR22-21-1623, State of Idaho vs. Chad Guy Daybell , have been violated. The Court has likewise 
witnessed no misconduct on any part of the media dur ing hearings in theses cases. The presence of 
media during the hearings has in no way interrupted those proceedings, and attending media have been 
respectful and professional.” State’s Brief on Vi deo and Photographic Coverage, Ex. at p. 3. Those 
observations are unsurprising and consistent with Rule  45’s carefully crafted regulations of the media’s 
conduct. Opening Mem. at pp. 7–8. 
Finally, Intervenors appreciate Mr. Kohberger’s concerns about how certain images have been 
used on social media. Prohibiting audio/visual cove rage will not eliminate the concern though, as those 
in the courtroom could publish similar statements  about their observations of Mr. Kohberger’s 
appearance and demeanor. To address Mr. Kohbe rger’s concerns, while still allowing impacted 
community members to view the proceedings, the Court could regulate how cameras capture Mr. Kohberger, by for example allowing only wider shots or limiting how close the camera can zoom. 
What is more, a few selective examples are not reason to deprive all the members of the impacted 
communities the opportunity to observe the proceedings  with their own eyes and ears. And again, once 
a jury is empaneled, the Court will certainly instruct  them not to consume any information related to 
the case outside of what is presented to them in the courtroom.  
Intervenors continue to request that the Court allow audio/visu al coverage consistent with 
Idaho Court Administrative Rule 45.
